Prediction is not proof, and simple usually wins

Two things every leader must hold at once. The first is a rough map of the model zoo - enough to know when a plain logistic regression is the right call and when a problem genuinely needs the heavy machinery. The second is the hardest line in all of machine learning: a model predicts, but it never proves what causes an outcome. Confuse the two and you will spend real budget chasing a correlation that moves nothing. This session gives you the map and draws the line - no code, just the judgement that keeps a good prediction from becoming a bad decision.

Part 0

The most expensive mistake in the room

A model tells Lumen Skincare that visitors who arrive from email convert far more often than average. The obvious move is to pour budget into email. It is also, often, the wrong move - because the model has spotted a pattern, not a lever. Email may simply be where Lumen's already-loyal customers happen to show up; shift the budget and you may get nothing. This session arms you against exactly that error, and gives you the map to pick the simplest model that could possibly work before anyone reaches for a neural network.

Part 1 · the map

The model zoo at a glance 9 min live

You do not need to know how each model works. You need to place them on two axes: how complex they are to run and trust, and how easily a human can read why they decided what they did. That map tells you which family to reach for - and which ones are a whole course of their own.

LiveStart simple3 min▶

The right first model is almost always the simplest one that could work - logistic regression for a yes/no, linear regression for a number. Not because it is the most powerful, but because it is honest about what it knows and cheap to reason about.

  • Linear / logistic regression - simple, fast, and interpretable. You can read the weights and see which features push the prediction. Always your baseline.
  • Decision trees - a flowchart of if/then rules a human can follow. Readable, but they overfit easily, memorising the training data unless kept shallow.

A simple model that everyone understands and that beats the baseline is worth more than a complex one no one can explain. Start here, prove there is signal, then earn the right to add complexity.

The leader's default When a team opens with "we'll use deep learning", ask "what did the logistic regression score?" If they skipped it, they skipped their own baseline - and they cannot tell you whether the fancy model is actually adding anything.
LiveWhen you actually need the heavy models3 min▶

The complex families exist for a reason - just not usually a first project's reason. Reach down-right on the map when a simple model has genuinely fallen short and the problem justifies the cost:

  • Ensembles (random forests, gradient boosting like XGBoost) - many trees combined. Often the strongest on tabular business data, at the cost of interpretability. Their own course: ensemble-methods.
  • Neural networks / deep learning - for huge datasets and unstructured inputs like images, audio and text. Powerful, data-hungry, and a black box. Their own course: deep-learning.
  • Clustering / k-means - unsupervised: no labels, it finds segments no one defined. A different job entirely, covered in unsupervised.

Naming these is enough for now. An intro course teaches the workflow and judgement; the heavy machinery is a deliberate next step, not a prerequisite.

Real world

Lumen's conversion problem is tabular checkout data with a modest signal. A logistic regression captures nearly all of it (ROC-AUC ~ 0.71); a deep neural network would add enormous complexity, demand far more data, and likely land in the same place - while nobody could explain a single decision to the marketing team. Simple wins, and it wins the trust too.

Part 2 · the hard line

Prediction is not causation 9 min live

Here is the line that separates a data-literate leader from a fooled one. A model exploits correlations to predict an outcome. It cannot tell you what would happen if you changed something. Predicting who will buy is not the same as knowing what would make them buy - and only one of those questions a model can answer.

LiveImportance is not a lever3 min▶

Models hand you a "feature importance" list - which inputs mattered most to the prediction. It is genuinely useful, and it is genuinely dangerous, because it is not a list of causes.

  • Importance means "predictive", not "causal". "Returning customer" is Lumen's second-strongest feature. But labelling someone returning causes nothing - it is a marker of a customer who was already loyal, not a lever you can pull.
  • Correlation gets its predictive power for free. A feature can ride along with the outcome without moving it. The model does not know or care about the difference; it only wants to predict.
  • The trap: reading a high-importance feature as an instruction ("make more customers 'returning'") and spending budget on it.
The one-line check For any important feature, ask: "If I could change this for a customer tomorrow, would the outcome actually move - or is it just a sign of who they already are?" If it is a sign, it is not a lever.
LiveWhen you need an experiment, not a model3 min▶

The moment a question contains the word "cause" - or its disguises, "would", "if we", "does X drive Y" - you have left prediction and entered causation. No model can answer it. You need to intervene and measure.

  • "Which visitors will buy?" - a prediction. A model is exactly right.
  • "Would a discount cause more of them to buy?" - a causal question. Only an experiment - randomise who gets the discount, compare the groups - can answer it.

This is the explicit bridge to the next course. learn-experimentation-with-phoebe is entirely about answering the causal questions that machine learning cannot. Knowing which of the two you are holding is the leader's job.

Real world

Lumen's model showed that email visitors convert more, so leadership shifted a chunk of budget toward email. Conversion did not move. The reason: email was not causing purchases - it was simply where Lumen's already-loyal buyers arrived. The model had read a correlation perfectly and told them nothing about cause. Only an A/B test - randomly withholding email from some loyal customers - would have revealed that the lever was a mirage.

Before Session 6

This week ◐ 25 min total

  • Split predict from cause. For one model in your org, write two sentences: "It predicts X" and "It does not prove X causes anything." Notice how often people quietly slide from the first to the second.
  • Find an experiment hiding as a model. Spot one decision your team wants to make ("should we do more of Y?") that actually needs a controlled test, not a prediction.
  • Pick the simplest tool. For a real use case, name the simplest model that could plausibly work - and justify anything more complex before you allow it.
  • Optional: find a past decision where a correlation was mistaken for a lever, and money moved with no result. Bring the story to Session 6.
Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · For a first conversion model on Lumen's tabular checkout data, which should you reach for first?

Start simple. Logistic regression is fast, interpretable, and sets the baseline that tells you whether any fancier model is actually adding value. On modest tabular signal, it often captures nearly everything.

2 · "Returning customer" is a top feature in Lumen's model. What does that prove?

Feature importance is about prediction, not causation. "Returning" is a marker of an already-loyal customer, not a lever you can pull. Reading importance as a cause is the expensive mistake.

3 · "Would a discount CAUSE more visitors to buy?" Why can't a model answer this?

Anything asking what a change would cause needs an intervention - randomise who gets the discount and compare groups. A model reads the world as it is; only an experiment changes it and measures the lift. That is the next course.
